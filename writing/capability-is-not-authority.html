WORKING NOTE · EVALUATION AUTHORITY

Capability is not authority.

An automated evaluator can be useful without being entitled to decide. That distinction is easy to lose because capability and authority are often expressed through the same interface.

The system returns a score. The score appears precise. A threshold turns it into pass or fail. The disposition triggers feedback, payment, access restriction, or rejection.

At that point, an empirical claim about model performance has silently become a governance decision.

The model did not make that transition. The organization did.

Capability is an empirical question

Capability asks:

How well does this exact configured instrument perform on a defined task distribution?

The instrument includes more than the base model. It includes:

  • prompt and criterion wording;
  • tools;
  • retrieval;
  • context construction;
  • evidence sources;
  • preprocessing;
  • sampling;
  • temperature and other inference settings;
  • result schema;
  • post-processing;
  • threshold;
  • abstention policy.

Change one of those materially and the performance claim may no longer transfer.

Capability should be measured on untouched, adjudicated data that resembles the deployment distribution—including difficult tails, long records, ambiguous cases, and the error types that matter.

Authority is an operating decision

Authority asks:

What action may this instrument influence, under what conditions, with what owner and remedy?

The answer depends on more than average accuracy. It depends on consequence severity, false-adverse risk, coverage, abstention, appeal, reversibility, auditability, distribution shift, human review capacity, legal and policy constraints, and accountability.

A system that is useful for ranking cases may be unfit to reject them. A system that can catch deterministic omissions may be unfit to interpret a subjective criterion. A system that drafts evidence-linked feedback may be unfit to restrict a worker's account.

Authority should be narrower than capability until evidence justifies expansion.

The record comes first

Before asking how accurate the evaluator is, ask whether the task is identifiable from the record.

If the correct decision depends on:

  • unrecorded intent;
  • unavailable environment state;
  • an external fact the system cannot retrieve;
  • an instruction that permits multiple reasonable interpretations;
  • proof that something is absent from a non-exhaustively searched record;

then no amount of calibration can turn the current input into a fully decidable task.

The honest result may be insufficient or contract-ambiguous.

Treating those states as model error misdiagnoses the problem. Treating them as worker fault is worse.

Measure by lane

One aggregate score hides the structure of the task. A review system may have distinct lanes:

  • required-field checks;
  • exact policy violations;
  • local claim-versus-evidence comparison;
  • long-record retrieval;
  • code correctness;
  • completeness;
  • severity;
  • instruction interpretation;
  • domain judgment.

Each lane has a different evidence contract and risk profile. Authority should be granted lane by lane, with separate performance, coverage, and false-adverse measurement.

Abstention is part of performance

A system that must answer every case may look more productive while producing avoidable error.

A useful abstention policy asks the system to decline when:

  • required evidence is missing;
  • the criterion is ambiguous;
  • retrieval coverage is inadequate;
  • the case falls outside calibration;
  • tool execution failed;
  • sources conflict;
  • the confidence signal is unvalidated;
  • the consequence exceeds the authorized lane.

Performance should be reported at a stated coverage. “95% accurate” means little without knowing which cases the system attempted, which it abstained from, and what happened in the hardest region.

Human review must be substantive

“Human in the loop” is not a sufficient control. The human must have:

  • the relevant record;
  • enough time;
  • the required expertise;
  • authority to disagree;
  • a clear standard;
  • access to the system's evidence and limitations;
  • a path to correct the underlying rule;
  • accountability for the final disposition.

Otherwise the human is a ceremonial approver for an automated decision.

A practical release ladder

A conservative deployment can expand in stages.

Stage 1 — Observe

Run in a measurement-only mode. Preserve findings and compare them with adjudicated decisions.

Stage 2 — Assist

Use the system for evidence retrieval, deterministic checks, and dossier assembly.

Stage 3 — Recommend

Allow calibrated lanes to produce nonbinding recommendations with visible evidence and abstention.

Stage 4 — Act narrowly

Authorize a specific, reversible action only where the exact lane has earned it and appeal remains available.

Stage 5 — Revalidate

Reassess after changes to the model, prompt, tools, evidence, criterion, distribution, or consequence.

The objective is not to keep automation weak. It is to let authority grow at the speed of evidence rather than the speed of confidence.

The practical standard

Before an automated finding receives consequential authority, ask:

  1. Is the task decidable from the authorized record?
  2. Is the criterion operational and versioned?
  3. Was the exact instrument evaluated?
  4. Does the evaluation represent deployment?
  5. What is performance at the stated coverage?
  6. What is the false-adverse risk?
  7. When does the system abstain?
  8. Who owns the decision?
  9. How can a person appeal?
  10. What changes force revalidation?

A system can be valuable long before it is allowed to decide.

Recognizing that is not anti-automation. It is how useful automation becomes accountable.
