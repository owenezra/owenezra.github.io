CURRENT WORK

AI data and evaluation program leadership.

I work at the boundary between a model-development objective and the data, evaluations, people, and operating systems required to pursue it.

Since February 2024, I have progressed from creating complex mathematics training data to reviewing contributor work, auditing reviewer decisions, defining standards and calibration, and leading project development, quality systems, program operations, and client collaboration.

My role is often translational. A researcher or client may begin with a capability goal, an emerging failure mode, or a broad request for expert data. Contributors need a task they can actually execute. Reviewers need criteria that can support consistent decisions. Operators need workflows, tooling, escalation, and metrics. Leaders need evidence that the system is producing something useful rather than merely something measurable.

I turn those layers into one operating program.

CAREER PROGRESSION

Each layer changed what I could see.

Expert data creation

I entered frontier-model work by producing difficult mathematics training data. This taught me how experts encounter a specification: which assumptions seem obvious to the author but not the worker, where examples overfit one interpretation, and how a technically correct answer can still fail an unstated expectation.

Contributor review

I moved into evaluating other contributors' work. This required distinguishing correctness from style, identifying the actual criterion, locating evidence, and communicating feedback that could improve the next attempt rather than merely justify a score.

Reviewer oversight

I then audited reviewer decisions. That layer revealed a different class of problem: inconsistent severity, unsupported findings, missed failures, ambiguous guidance, and disagreement caused by the evaluation system itself.

Standards and systems

I began translating those recurring patterns into calibration materials, technical standards, functionality tests, review workflows, escalation paths, and acceptance criteria.

Project and program operations

My scope expanded into project development, program management, client collaboration, quality leadership, and broader operations. The work became less about deciding one task and more about keeping the entire decision system coherent while requirements, capabilities, people, and evidence changed.

WHY EDUCATION MATTERS

Human-data systems are also learning systems.

Before AI, I spent roughly 15 years in physics, mathematics, STEM education, instructional design, technical training, and program leadership.

That background is not an unrelated first career. Human-data operations depend on many of the same disciplines:

  • define what success means;
  • make tacit knowledge explicit;
  • sequence difficult work;
  • create examples and counterexamples;
  • assess understanding;
  • diagnose why people disagree;
  • calibrate evaluators;
  • provide feedback;
  • revise the system when the evidence shows that the original design is not working.

A contributor instruction is a form of curriculum. A calibration set is a form of assessment. Reviewer variance is a diagnostic signal. Program quality depends on what people understand, not merely what the documentation says.

WORKING STYLE

The same six questions, in order.

Whatever the domain, the sequence stays the same.

Start with the claim

What is the system supposed to establish, improve, or decide?

Define the evidence

What observable record could support or contradict that claim?

Make the workflow executable

What do researchers, contributors, reviewers, operators, and tools each need to do?

Test before scaling

What pilot, baseline, or calibration would reveal whether the design works?

Preserve uncertainty

Where does the record remain insufficient or the rule ambiguous?

Assign ownership

Who can change the specification, decide the case, authorize the system, and respond when it fails?
